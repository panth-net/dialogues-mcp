# Francisco de Miranda Archive

What this corpus is: the self-made archive of Francisco de Miranda (1750-1816), the Caracas-born "Precursor" of Spanish American independence, who bound the paper trail of his own life into 63 volumes he called Colombeia — travel diaries, military records, and correspondence with everyone from Catherine the Great to Alexander Hamilton. The genre is deliberate self-documentation: Miranda filed passports, letters, newspaper cuttings, and day-by-day diaries as evidence for the cause and for posterity. The archive was published in Caracas between 1929 and 1950 as the Archivo del General Miranda, in 24 volumes.

## Where to start

- Ask how one man came to fight in the American, French, and Spanish American revolutions — and why he filed the passports, letters, and clippings to prove every step of it.
- Ask what he thought of the brand-new United States as he toured it in 1783-1784 — he dined with its founders and graded everything he saw, down to the roadside inns; the diary is in old Spanish, read and translated for you.
- Ask how a Caracas-born conspirator ended up received at Catherine the Great's court — and under her protection.
- Ask about the night his own officers seized him at La Guaira and let him fall into Spanish hands — the surrender he had signed to end the fight, they read as betrayal.

The strongest material clusters:

- Three revolutions in one career: a Spanish officer at Pensacola in 1781 during the American Revolutionary War; a general of the French Revolutionary army in 1792-1793 — at Valmy, before Antwerp, tried and acquitted after Neerwinden, his name later engraved on the Arc de Triomphe; then the organizer of Spanish American independence.
- The travel diaries, 1783-1789: the young United States (conversations with Washington, Hamilton, Knox), Britain, Europe, the Ottoman lands, and Russia, where Catherine the Great received him in 1786-1787 — an unmatched outsider's record of the Atlantic world.
- The 1806 Leander expedition: sailing from New York, the loss of the schooners at Puerto Cabello, the landing at La Vela de Coro in August 1806 under his tricolor flag, and the withdrawal when the province did not rise.
- The First Venezuelan Republic, 1810-1812: return with Bolívar, the declaration of independence of 5 July 1811, the earthquake of March 1812, his emergency dictatorship, the capitulation he signed at San Mateo in July 1812 — and the night at La Guaira when Bolívar and fellow officers, reading the capitulation as betrayal, seized him and let him fall into Spanish hands. He died in the La Carraca prison at Cádiz on 14 July 1816.
- The archive's own odyssey: shipped out of La Guaira in 1812 aboard a British warship, carried to England, forgotten among Lord Bathurst's papers, found at Cirencester in 1922, bought by Venezuela in 1926, printed from 1929.

What downloads: eight verified satellite volumes of the archive, not the Archivo itself — the 1928 Robertson edition of the 1783-1784 US travel diary (Spanish), the 1794 Dumouriez correspondence (English/French), Antepara's 1810 South American Emancipation, Biggs's 1811 participant account of the Leander expedition, the 1889 Miranda en la revolución francesa documents, Rojas's 1884 documentary biography, the 1931 Fragments diary translation (English), and the 1806 Coro proclamation from es.wikisource. The 24-volume Archivo del General Miranda (Caracas, 1929-1950) exists digitized only at HathiTrust, which blocks programmatic download, so it stays a catalog pointer.

How to search: use period vocabulary and proper names — and in three languages, because Miranda kept papers in Spanish, French, and English. Search for `Coro`, `Leander`, `capitulación`, `Pensacola`, `Catalina`, `Hamilton`, not modern abstractions.

When answering, quote the fetched document, cite it, and separate evidence from interpretation: the archive is Miranda's own case file, curated by a man building his legend, so what he kept — and did not keep — is itself a fact to weigh. Two of the downloaded volumes are not Miranda's voice at all: Biggs is a disillusioned expedition volunteer and Rojas a biographer quoting documents — name the speaker when citing them. Known limitations: the downloaded set covers satellites of the archive, not the Archivo's 24 volumes, so most of the correspondence mass is absent; OCR of old print garbles eighteenth-century typefaces (long-s as f), accents, and French passages; the First Republic of 1810-1812 is thinly covered; and editorial prefaces share the text stream with the documents.
