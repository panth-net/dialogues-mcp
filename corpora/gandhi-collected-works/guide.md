# Gandhi Collected Works

## What this corpus is

This corpus is built on the Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi (CWMG), the roughly 100-volume English edition (Publications Division, Government of India, 1956-1994) that prints everything recoverable that Gandhi wrote or said, in chronological order: letters, petitions, newspaper articles from Indian Opinion, Young India, Navajivan and Harijan, speeches, interviews, telegrams, and statements to courts and governments. The genre matters: this is a day-by-day working record of campaigns, not a memoir. Vol. 1 opens in 1884; the edition runs to his death in January 1948.

The curated selection downloads five volumes as OCR text from openly hosted archive.org scans of the Publications Division print edition:

- Vol. 1 (1884 - June 1896): the London-trained lawyer in Durban, the 1894 franchise petitions, founding the Natal Indian Congress.
- Vol. 8 (January - August 1908): the Transvaal satyagraha at full intensity — the first jail term, the Smuts settlement and its breach, the burning of registration certificates.
- Vol. 10 (November 1909 - March 1911): the complete English Hind Swaraj (Indian Home Rule), written aboard the Kildonan Castle, plus the Tolstoy Farm months.
- Vol. 43 (March - June 1930): the Salt March from Sabarmati to Dandi (12 March - 6 April), the breach of the salt law, mass arrests, his own arrest on 5 May.
- Vol. 44 (July - December 1930): the Yeravda prison letters that carried civil disobedience through the rest of 1930.

Two volumes remain catalog pointers on the Gandhi Heritage Portal, whose per-volume PDF download is session-gated: vol. 9 (September 1908 - November 1909) and vol. 76 (April - December 1942, Quit India — the "do or die" speech of 8 August 1942, arrest the next morning, detention in the Aga Khan Palace). No open mirror of either has been verified, so questions about Quit India cannot be answered from downloaded text; download those PDFs manually in a browser and import them with `dialogues import-folder`.

## Where to start

- Ask to read the letter Gandhi sent the Viceroy before breaking the salt law — it begins "Dear Friend" and tells him exactly what is coming.
- Ask to follow the Salt March week by week in his own articles and letters — you watch him drill thousands of people in discipline before a single law is broken.
- Ask why Hind Swaraj claims that modern civilization itself is the disease — the whole argument written at sea on the voyage back from London, and never retracted.
- Ask what he did from prison after his arrest — the Yeravda letters kept the campaign alive by post through the rest of 1930.

## How to search

Search for the concrete source vocabulary of campaigns: proper names (Smuts, Botha, Irwin, Linlithgow, Patel, Nehru, Kasturba), places (Durban, Phoenix, Tolstoy Farm, Sabarmati, Dandi, Yeravda), and Gandhi's own working terms (satyagraha, indentured, registration certificates, khadi, charkha, harijan, swaraj, salt tax, civil disobedience). Avoid modern abstractions — search "passive resistance" and "satyagraha", not "protest movement".

## How to answer

Fetch passages before making claims, quote the text, and cite volume and document date. Keep the document type visible: a private letter to Kasturba, a Young India editorial, and a statement to the Viceroy do different work even in the same week. Distinguish Gandhi's words from the editors' source notes and appendices printed in the volumes. If a question falls in a period no downloaded volume covers, say so — the selection is five volumes out of about a hundred, with 1911-1929 and everything after 1930 absent, and interpretation should not stretch beyond the downloaded evidence.

## Limitations

The CWMG text is itself a translation in many places — Gandhi wrote heavily in Gujarati and Hindi, and the English edition renders those items in translation, marked by the editors. The portal PDFs carry an OCR-era text layer with occasional errors. The edition's completeness varies by period, and later revisions of CWMG were controversial; the portal hosts the original edition. State these limitations when they matter to an answer.
