# Libanius Letters

What this corpus is: the letters of Libanius of Antioch (314-c.393), sophist and professor of Greek rhetoric, of which some 1,544 survive — the largest letter collection to come down from antiquity. The genre is the late-antique letter of connection: recommendations for students and clients, requests to governors and prefects, thanks, complaints, condolences, and the constant maintenance of friendship (philia) that made the eastern Roman elite run. Libanius taught at Constantinople and Nicomedia before holding the chair of rhetoric at Antioch from 354; his correspondents ranged from town councillors to the emperor Julian, and his school trained the officials and bishops of a generation.

## Where to start

- Ask how a professor got a student his first job in the fourth century — the text is rough OCR, but you can still watch Libanius place a pupil among the advocates with one well-aimed letter.
- Ask what friendship cost when it was also currency — these letters run on favors owed, thanked, and pointedly called in, affection and account-keeping in the same breath.
- Ask about the letters around the emperor Julian — a pagan professor's hopes rose with one man and died with him in Persia.
- Ask how an old man complains — one late letter counts fever and grief as twin evils, and illness runs through the collection as steadily as flattery.

The strongest material clusters:

- Recommendation as a social machine: letters that move a young man from Antioch's schoolroom into imperial service, or beg a governor to go easy on a client — the paper mechanics of patronage.
- Julian: the letters around the pagan emperor's residence in Antioch in 362-363, the shared hopes of a Hellenic restoration, and the aftermath of Julian's death in Persia in 363.
- Antioch itself: the city council (curia) and its burdens, festivals and the Olympic games of Antioch, rivalries with other teachers, students poached and defended — the world that also produced his Oration 1 autobiography and, later, the Riot of the Statues crisis of 387, when the city smashed the imperial images and awaited Theodosius's punishment (the context of Orations 19-23, which are not in this capsule's letter volumes).
- Pagan twilight: a Hellene under Christian emperors, watching temples close — the world of his plea For the Temples (Oration 30) — while former students rise in a Christianizing empire. The letters cluster in two runs, roughly 355-365 and 388-393, with a long gap between.

How to search: search for Greek source vocabulary — names (Ἰουλιανός), roles (σοφιστής, ῥήτωρ, ἄρχων), and the vocabulary of favor (χάρις, φίλος, παιδεία) — in multiple inflected forms. And read this bluntly: the searchable text is OCR of Foerster's 1921-1922 Teubner volumes, and polytonic Greek OCR is heavily degraded. Recall will be poor; the Latin apparatus criticus is garbled into pseudo-Greek in the text stream; letter numbers may be mangled. Treat every hit as a pointer to the printed page, open the scan at the Internet Archive record, and verify before believing.

When answering, quote only Greek that has been checked against the scan (or the Perseids reading environment), cite the passage, and keep evidence separate from interpretation — a recommendation letter's praise is genre convention as much as fact. Known limitations: OCR corruption throughout; no complete rights-clear English translation of the letters exists (modern selections are in copyright), so translations in answers are made at answer time; and the collection preserves what Libanius's circle kept, which flatters the professor's own centrality.
