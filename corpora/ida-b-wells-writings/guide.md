# Ida B. Wells Writings

What this corpus is: the three anti-lynching pamphlets Ida B. Wells published between 1892 and 1900 — *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* (1892), *A Red Record* (1895), and *Mob Rule in New Orleans* (1900). The genre is investigative journalism assembled into argument: documented cases of racial terror, with names, dates, places, and statistics, marshaled as evidence against the public justifications for lynching. This is not memoir or reflection. Wells was born enslaved in Holly Springs, Mississippi, in 1862, co-owned the Memphis *Free Speech*, and turned to this work after a mob lynched her friend Thomas Moss with Calvin McDowell and Henry Stewart at the People's Grocery on 9 March 1892. When her 21 May 1892 editorial attacked the "old thread bare lie" behind lynching, a mob destroyed the *Free Speech* office and made her return to Memphis a death sentence. The pamphlets are what she published instead, from New York and Chicago.

Take the subject seriously: searches in this corpus will surface graphic primary accounts of torture, burning, shooting, and mob murder. These accounts are the evidentiary core, not incidental content. Wells deliberately reprinted them — largely from white-owned newspapers such as the Chicago *Tribune*, the Memphis papers, and the New Orleans *Times-Democrat* and *Picayune* — precisely so the record could not be dismissed as partisan exaggeration. Treat each case description as documentation used in an argument, and keep Wells's own analysis distinct from the press accounts she quotes as evidence.

## Where to start

- Ask why the lynching of her friend Thomas Moss at the People's Grocery turned a Memphis editor into an investigator — that case is where this record begins.
- Ask how Wells made her case impossible to dismiss — she built her tables and case files from white-owned papers like the Chicago *Tribune*, her opponents' own sources.
- Ask what publishing the truth cost her — one May 1892 editorial brought a mob that destroyed her press and made returning to Memphis a death sentence.
- Ask what she believed would actually stop lynching — investigation, publicity, organized boycott, and "the appeal to the white man's pocket."

The strongest material clusters:

- Case documentation: named victims, dated incidents, and *The Red Record*'s tabulated statistics of lynchings for 1892-1894, compiled from the Chicago *Tribune*, classed by the "alleged causes" given at the time.
- The "usual charge": her demonstration that the rape accusation was a pretext — fewer than one-third of recorded lynchings even alleged it — and her dangerous documentation of consensual relationships in "The Black and White of It."
- Economics, boycott, and press: the People's Grocery as economic competition, the Memphis streetcar boycott, emigration to Oklahoma, "the appeal to the white man's pocket," and the destruction of her own press.
- Self-defense and remedy: the Winchester rifle passage in *Southern Horrors*, the Robert Charles affair in New Orleans in July 1900, and the closing chapters on investigation, publicity, and organized action.

How to search: search for concrete source vocabulary — victim and place names (Moss, McDowell, Stewart, Memphis, Paris Texas, New Orleans, Robert Charles), the period's own terms (lynch law, mob, outrage, alleged, colored, Afro-American), and institutional names (Free Speech, Chicago Tribune, People's Grocery). Modern terms like "racism" or "white supremacy" will miss most of the text.

How to answer: quote the fetched passages, cite them, and distinguish three layers — the reprinted press account, Wells's documentation of it, and her interpretation and argument. Do not soften what the sources describe, and do not present a reprinted mob-newspaper account's framing as fact; Wells herself often quotes such accounts to expose their assumptions. Known limitations: three pamphlets cannot represent her full journalism, her diaries, or her later Chicago organizing; the statistics stop at the dates of publication; and absence of a case from these pages is not evidence it did not happen.
