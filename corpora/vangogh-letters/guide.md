# Corpus Guide

## What this corpus is

This corpus is for locally supplied letters by Vincent van Gogh. It may contain translations, transcriptions, editorial metadata, or user-exported files depending on what the user imports.

Treat the letters as correspondence, not as a database of biographical facts. They are intimate, practical, artistic, financial, spiritual, and social documents. They often move quickly between work, money, family, illness, faith, friendship, weather, materials, reading, and daily logistics.

## Where to start

- Ask about the letter Vincent sent Theo from coal-mining country after a long silence between the brothers — he describes himself as a bird in a cage that other birds call an idler, certain there is something in him he cannot yet show.
- Ask how he talked to Theo about money in the years when nothing sold — rent, paint, models, and the stubborn insistence that the work would one day justify what it cost.
- Ask what he was preparing in the yellow house before Gauguin arrived — down to the big yellow sunflowers he planned for the white walls of his friend's room.
- Ask what he told Theo he was painting in his last weeks at Auvers — immense stretches of wheatfields under turbulent skies, in which he tried to express sadness and extreme loneliness.

## How to talk with this corpus

Attend to recipient, date, place, language, and phase of life. A letter to Theo about money is not the same kind of document as a letter to Bernard about art theory or a family letter about practical arrangements.

Avoid collapsing the whole life into one stereotype. Do not turn every passage into a symptom, a masterpiece origin story, or a tragedy. Let ordinary logistics, self-justification, humor, practical craft, ambition, and uncertainty remain visible.

## How to search

Start with concrete words that might appear in letters: names, materials, colors, places, money, work, illness, weather, family, faith, and art terms. Search by person, theme, genre, and chronology, then fetch full passages before interpreting.

If metadata exists, use date, recipient, place, and language in the answer. If metadata is missing, say so rather than inventing it.

## How to answer

Quote selectively from fetched passages. Separate direct quotation from interpretive summary. Explain repeated concerns across letters when multiple fetched passages support the pattern.

Mention translation, editorial, and selection issues when relevant. If the local import is incomplete, say the answer is limited to the imported files.

A good answer should answer the user directly, use fetched evidence, quote selectively, cite every source passage, explain old or strange language, distinguish fact from interpretation, mention source bias or gaps, suggest next questions when useful, and avoid fake certainty.

## How to bring this corpus to life

Look for recurring worries about work, money, illness, faith, friendship, and materials; practical constraints around rent, models, canvas, weather, travel, and letters; repeated metaphors of color, light, nature, and consolation; contradictions between confidence and doubt; what Vincent assumes Theo or other recipients already know; what is left unsaid; changes across places and periods; and moments of tension, intimacy, comedy, fear, pride, fatigue, or confusion.

## What to watch for

- Over-psychologizing from a single passage.
- Treating later fame as if it explains every sentence.
- Ignoring recipient, date, place, and practical circumstances.
- Treating translations as exact access to the original language.
- Mistaking search silence in a partial local import for silence in all letters.
