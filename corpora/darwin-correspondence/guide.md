# Darwin Correspondence and Working Science

Use this corpus to see theory forming through questions, observations, experiments, illness, family rhythms, correspondent networks, hesitation, and revision.

## Where to start

- Ask what Darwin confided to Hooker in 1844 after years of silence — admitting species were not immutable felt, he wrote, "like confessing a murder."
- Ask what he set down about his daughter Annie a few days after she died at ten — a private account of her ways, written for no one but the family.
- Ask what he did in 1858 when Wallace's letter showed a stranger arriving at his theory — he began a letter giving up twenty years of priority.
- Ask how he kept working while, in his own words, he never passed "a day without much discomfort" — decades of illness sit underneath nearly every letter.

## What this corpus is

This corpus has two layers.

- **The Francis Darwin edited collections (bulk searchable core).** Public-domain volumes edited by Darwin's son Francis (with A. C. Seward for *More Letters*): *The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin* (2 volumes), *More Letters of Charles Darwin* (2 volumes), and *The Autobiography of Charles Darwin*. These give thousands of pages of letters and reminiscence spanning roughly 1809 to 1882, including the growth and public reception of the *Origin of Species*. They are the main thing to search when you want to actually chat with the material.
- **The Darwin Correspondence Project (DCP) letter items.** Twelve individually curated modern-edition letters (to and from Hooker, Lyell, Asa Gray, and Wallace) that mark specific hinge moments, such as the 1844 "confessing a murder" letter and the 1858 Wallace priority crisis.

Read the two layers together. The DCP items are precise, modern-edited single letters. The Francis Darwin volumes are broad but were shaped by Victorian family editing: material was sometimes softened, abridged, or omitted, especially on religion and personal conflict. Where the two layers cover the same moment, treat the modern DCP letter as the more reliable text and the edited volume as context.

Do not read every note as if later Darwinism was already complete. Ask what problem Darwin or a correspondent is trying to solve at that moment.

## How to search

Search for concrete scientific vocabulary and named correspondents rather than abstract theory words. Good starting points: `natural selection`, `species`, `variation`, `Hooker`, `Lyell`, `Gray`, `Wallace`, `pigeon`, `orchid`, `experiment`, `distribution`, `doubt`. See `search-rules.md` for per-path vocabulary and for how to search by correspondent, date, and path. Because the edited volumes are large, start broad, then narrow to a correspondent or date once you find a promising thread.

## How to answer

Letters, marginalia, and observations are working evidence. Published conclusions are only one endpoint. Fetch enough passages to see the exchange, quote selectively, cite the source item and date, and keep evidence separate from interpretation.

## Limitations

The Francis Darwin volumes carry the biases of a Victorian family edition: softened tone, omitted or abridged passages, and thin coverage of religious doubt and private disagreement. Dates on the edited collections are coverage spans, not exact per-letter dates, and *More Letters* is arranged by subject rather than strict chronology. Some correspondence and marginalia referenced by the project are not included here. When the corpus is thin on a question, say what is missing rather than overclaiming.
