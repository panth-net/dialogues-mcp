# Walt Whitman Papers

## What this corpus is

Use this corpus to meet Whitman in prose: the letters and working notebooks behind the poet of Leaves of Grass. The genre mix matters. Letters written from wartime Washington to his mother Louisa Van Velsor Whitman report hospital rounds, money, rent, and his brother George at the front in the 51st New York; letters to wounded soldiers like Thomas P. Sawyer say directly what the poems say obliquely; the postwar letters to the horsecar conductor Peter Doyle ("Dear Pete", "my darling boy") are the fullest record of Whitman's affections; and the late letters from 328 Mickle Street in Camden to Richard Maurice Bucke, Horace Traubel, William Sloane Kennedy, and his sister Hannah show a paralyzed man managing fame, editions, and dying. Around them sit five working notebooks, including the famous "Talbot Wilson" notebook (c. 1847-1854) where trial lines for Leaves of Grass first appear, and the 1863 hospital notebook "from Hookers command," with bedside notes taken in pencil in Ward E. The corpus also holds Ralph Waldo Emerson's letter of 21 July 1855 greeting the unknown author "at the beginning of a great career," plus Emerson's January 1863 introductions supporting Whitman's search for work in Washington.

The strongest material clusters:

- 1863-1864 Washington: Armory Square and other hospitals, wounded and dying soldiers named bed by bed, the Patent Office, Fredericksburg and Brooklyn family news in letters to his mother.
- The Sawyer and Doyle letters: affection, worry, instruction, and loneliness written to working-class men, essential evidence for Whitman's ideal of comradeship and for the Calamus questions.
- The making of Leaves of Grass: pre-1855 notebook drafts ("I am the poet of the body / And I am the poet of the soul" territory), Emerson's 1855 greeting, and Whitman's lifelong management of his book through publishers and editions.
- Late Camden, 1891-1892: the deathbed edition, daily symptoms, visitors, and the machinery of a literary reputation run from a sickroom.

## Where to start

- Ask what Whitman saw on his hospital rounds — the 1863 notebook takes down wounded soldiers name by name and bed by bed, in pencil, at the bedside.
- Ask what he told his mother about the war — the letters home carry the wards, money worries, and news of George at the front in the same breath.
- Ask what Emerson's letter did to an unknown poet — "I greet you at the beginning of a great career," and the letter itself is here, word for word.
- Ask how Whitman loved Peter Doyle — in one 1869 letter he writes "Dear Pete, dear son, my darling boy" and talks him back from his darkest thoughts.

## How to talk with this corpus

Keep recipient and year visible; a letter to his mother, a letter to Pete, and a letter to Bucke do different work in different decades. Do not read the letters as artless: Whitman managed his image even with intimates, and he reused hospital material in Memoranda During the War and Specimen Days. Notebook entries are drafts and field notes, not settled statements — treat crossed-out, fragmentary phrasing as process evidence.

## How to search

Search with concrete source vocabulary: names (Louisa, George, Jeff, Pete, Doyle, Sawyer, Bucke, Traubel, O'Connor, Emerson), places (Armory Square, Washington, Brooklyn, Camden, Fredericksburg, Culpeper), and period words (hospital, ward, wounded, sick, regiment, furlough, horsecars, Leaves of Grass). Whitman's spelling and dashes are informal; try short distinctive words before long phrases.

## How to answer

Fetch passages before making claims, quote Whitman's own sentences, and cite each letter or notebook by its Whitman Archive ID, title, and date. Separate evidence from interpretation, especially on contested questions like Whitman's sexuality, where the letters give affectionate language whose interpretation remains genuinely debated. Note that each document here is an individual TEI transcription: editorial apparatus is stripped in indexing, manuscript deletions and insertions may read flatly as running text, and uncertainty about dates appears in brackets in the titles. This is a curated selection of a few dozen documents from an archive of thousands of letters — absence here is not absence in Whitman's surviving papers, and broad claims should say the corpus is a sample.
