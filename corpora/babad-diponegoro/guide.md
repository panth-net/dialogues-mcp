# Babad Diponegoro

What this corpus is: the Babad Diponegoro, the autobiographical chronicle of Prince Diponegoro of Yogyakarta (1785-1855), who led the Java War of 1825-1830, the largest anti-colonial war Java ever saw. He dictated and composed it in Javanese verse in 1831-1832, in exile in Manado, within two years of his capture. The genre is the babad, the Javanese chronicle-poem: sung macapat metres carrying genealogy, prophecy, dreams, and battlefield events in one continuous fabric. It was inscribed in the UNESCO Memory of the World register in 2013. Origin matters here: this is the loser of the war telling his own story, from exile, for his descendants.

## Where to start

The chronicle is sung Javanese verse — ask in English and the AI translates as you go.

- Ask what happened at the negotiating table in Magelang. He came to talk during Ramadan and left under arrest, and this whole poem is what he made of that betrayal, dictated in exile within two years.
- Ask about his vision of the Just King. A figure stands on a mountaintop outshining the sun, too bright for the prince to look at — and he took it as his commission.
- Ask about the cave at Selarong, where the rising gathered. Men rode in to be received and sent out with new battle titles — a court in exile before the exile even began.
- Ask about the name Ngabdulkamid. Mid-war, his followers raised him up as sultan and ruler of Islam in the land of Java, and the new name announces a man remaking himself to fit a prophecy.

The strongest material clusters:

- The outbreak: the dispute over sacred land and the Yogyakarta court's drift under Dutch control, and the road stakes the colonial administration drove across Diponegoro's estate at Tegalrejo in 1825 — the provocation that turned grievance into war. Tegalrejo was burned; Diponegoro withdrew to the cave at Selarong and raised Java.
- The guerrilla years, 1825-1830: a war that cost some two hundred thousand Javanese lives, with the religious adviser Kyai Mojo, the young commander Sentot Ali Basah, and the Dutch under General de Kock slowly strangling the rising with the benteng fortress system. Kyai Mojo was taken in 1828; Sentot came over to the Dutch in 1829.
- Capture and exile: invited to Magelang to negotiate in March 1830, under what he understood as safe conduct during Ramadan, Diponegoro was arrested at the table and shipped away — to Manado, where he wrote this babad, then in 1833 to Fort Rotterdam in Makassar, where he died in 1855.
- The babad fabric itself: visions of the Ratu Adil, the Just King, and encounters with the spirit queen of the Southern Ocean; his adopted name Ngabdulkamid; descent traced from the sultans of Mataram; Islamic devotion braided with Javanese court culture.

How to search: the downloaded text is a romanized Latin-script transliteration of the LOr 6547 manuscript, one canto (pupuh) per document, 41 of 43 cantos (cantos 7 and 12 are broken in the source). Search with source vocabulary — Javanese names and places (Tegalrejo, Selarong, Magelang, Mataram), titles (Sultan, Basah, Kyai), and macapat metre names that head the cantos — and expect unstable romanized spelling, including e-with-caron/breve diacritics (ě) in this transliteration: try `amedhar` and `amědhar` style variants.

When answering, quote the fetched verse, cite it by pupuh, and keep evidence and interpretation apart: the babad is testimony and self-presentation at once, composed after defeat, with prophecy and event deliberately interwoven.
