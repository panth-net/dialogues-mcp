# Yi Sun-sin War Diary (Nanjung Ilgi)

This capsule downloads the diary's Classical Chinese text as printed in the public-domain 1795 royal compilation Yi Chungmugong jeonseo: books 5-7 (1592-1596) from Korean Wikisource and the thirteen surviving months of book 8 (April 1597 - November 1598, including Myeongnyang and the final Noryang-eve entries) from the ITKC Korean classics database. What downloads is the original hanmun, not a translation. Two records stay pointers: the national heritage record for the autograph manuscript (whose draft books contain entries the 1795 redaction omits) and the catalog entry for the copyrighted 1977 English translation, which must be user-supplied.

What this corpus is: Nanjung Ilgi ("Diary in the Midst of War"), the day-by-day record Admiral Yi Sun-sin (1545-1598) kept from early 1592 to just before his death in late 1598, through the Imjin War — Japan's invasions of Korea under Hideyoshi. The genre is a commander's private daily log, seven surviving manuscript books written in brush-drawn Classical Chinese, and its origin is the admiral's own desk aboard ship and in camp. It is National Treasure of Korea No. 76, kept at the Hyeonchungsa shrine in Asan, and was inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2013.

## Where to start

The diary is in Classical Chinese — ask in English and the AI reads the entries to you.

- Ask what he wrote the day before Myeongnyang, with thirteen ships against a fleet of hundreds. The entry is pure logbook — clear weather, moved the anchorage so the strait would not be at his back — and its calm is the most unnerving thing in it.
- Ask about his mother. Between fleet drills he sends her abalone and salted fish, frets when no word comes, and records a visit to her in a single line.
- Ask about the dream he wrote down the day the news of his son came. His horse stumbled into a stream and his youngest boy seemed to catch him; he woke asking what it could mean, and by evening he knew.
- Ask what an ordinary day at war looks like. Weather first — clear, rain — then rounds of archery, two men beheaded for raising a false alarm, worry over his mother, all in the same flat brush.

The strongest material clusters:

- Command at sea: fleet drills, archery practice, scouting reports, the turtle ships first sent into action in 1592, the crane-wing formation that destroyed the Japanese fleet off Hansan Island in August 1592.
- The court against the commander: the intrigue that had Yi arrested in early 1597 for refusing to sail into a reported trap, his imprisonment and torture in Seoul, demotion to serve as a common soldier, and reinstatement after Won Gyun lost almost the entire fleet at Chilcheollyang that July.
- Myeongnyang, October 1597: thirteen ships against a Japanese fleet of roughly 133 in the narrow strait — the most extreme of the victories, fought weeks after his reinstatement.
- The last campaign: the combined operations of 1598 and his death at Noryang in December 1598, shot at the moment of victory; the diary breaks off days before.
- The man between battles: terse entries that open with the weather, notes on illness and the lingering pain of torture, executions of deserters ordered without comment, dreams of his mother and grief at her death in 1597, and the entry recording the death of his young son Myeon in a Japanese raid that same autumn.

How to search: the downloaded text is Classical Chinese; write terms naturally (`鳴梁`, `龜船` — the engine handles tokenization). Search for concrete source vocabulary: weather words (晴, 雨), place names (閑山, 鳴梁, 露梁), people (元均), ship and weapon terms, family words (母, 葂). If you import a Korean or English translation yourself, search it in that language. The entries are short; a single day is often one or two lines, so fetch neighboring days for context.

How to answer: quote the fetched entry, cite its date, and separate the diary's evidence from later legend. The diary is the admiral's own account, written in fatigue and grief and anger at Won Gyun and the court; treat its judgments as testimony, not verdicts. Note the limitations plainly: the downloaded text is the 1795 jeonseo redaction, which trims and omits entries preserved in the autograph draft books; any English rendering you give of a fetched hanmun passage is your own working translation and should be labeled as such; dates are lunar-calendar dates; and there are gaps — the diary is silent around the 1597 imprisonment and for most of early 1598.
