# Corpus Guide

## What this corpus is

This corpus is for Jane Austen's selected surviving letters, especially as practical correspondence rather than literary relics. The letters show family news, illness, travel, clothing, money, naval careers, publication, jokes, irritation, attention to manners, and sharp social observation.

The corpus is also a corpus of gaps. Many letters do not survive, and public-domain editions are selected and edited.

## Where to start

- Ask about the day her "own darling child" arrived from London — her first copy of Pride and Prejudice, read aloud that evening to a guest who never suspected the author was in the room.
- Ask what she told her niece about single women and money — being poor, she says flatly, is "one very strong argument in favor of matrimony."
- Ask what she thought of marrying a good man you do not love — "anything is to be preferred or endured" rather than that.
- Ask what kind of joke she allowed herself with her sister — a neighbour's stillbirth gets one deadpan line at the husband's expense.

## How to talk with this corpus

Do not use the letters to impersonate Austen or to claim full access to her private life. Treat them as surviving, edited evidence of a person writing to family and acquaintances under ordinary constraints.

Keep the small things small. Food, gowns, visits, carriage plans, weather, health, money, and family logistics are not filler; they are part of the mental world.

## How to search

Start with concrete terms: Cassandra, Fanny, Henry, Frank, Edward, mother, sister, illness, money, gown, ball, visit, Bath, Chawton, publication, novel, proof, fame, joke, laugh, dear.

For questions about "how she thought," search for practical verbs and social judgments: suppose, expect, think, hope, afraid, sorry, glad, vexed, civil, impertinent, ridiculous, pleasant.

## How to answer

Cite fetched passages and identify the edition. Say when a conclusion depends on a selected letter collection rather than the full surviving manuscript record.

Do not overread fiction back into the letters. It is fair to compare habits of social observation, but fetched letters should drive the answer.

Some chunks include front matter, transcriber notes, or Brabourne/Woolsey editorial framing. Use those for source limitations, not as Austen's own thought. For claims about Austen's mind, fetch letter passages with date or recipient context where possible.

## What to watch for

- Mistaking editorial selection for the complete surviving archive.
- Treating family chatter as trivial.
- Treating every joke as transparent self-revelation.
- Reading novels directly into letters without evidence.
- Ignoring missing letters, destroyed letters, and the limits of public-domain editions.
