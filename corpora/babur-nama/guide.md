# The Baburnama

Use this corpus to read the founder of the Mughal Empire narrating his own life. The genre is royal autobiography, and what this corpus is in practice: Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur (1483-1530) kept a first-person record in Chagatai Turki, and the searchable text is Annette Beveridge's public-domain English translation (1912-1921), made principally from the Haydarabad Codex — a translation, not the Chagatai original.

## Where to start

- Ask about the melon that made an emperor weep. Deep in conquered Hindustan, someone brought Babur a melon from home, and cutting it up undid him — he never saw Fergana again.
- Ask how he lost Samarkand twice before he was twenty. He calls those years the "throneless times" and writes about wandering with a shrinking band of followers as plainly as another man would list expenses.
- Ask how his father died. Babur was eleven when the dovecote at Akhsi collapsed — "flew, with his pigeons and their house, and became a falcon" — and the kingdom landed on him at once.
- Ask what he did in the days before Khanua, a battle he had real reason to fear losing. He renounced wine and had his gold and silver drinking cups broken up — the vow of a man getting right with God first.

The strongest material clusters:

- Fergana and exile, 1494-1504: his father 'Umar Shaikh dies when the dovecote at Akhsi collapses ("flew, with his pigeons and their house, and became a falcon"), Babur inherits Fergana at eleven, takes and loses Samarkand, and is driven out by Shaibaq (Shaibani) Khan and the Auzbegs into the "throneless times" with a shrinking band of followers.
- The Kabul kingdom, 1504-1525: taking Kabul from Muqim Arghun, raids toward Ghazni and the Indian frontier, the winter crossing of the mountain passes, the 1506 visit to the dazzling court of Herat, and gardens laid out — above all the Bagh-i-Wafa, the Garden of Fidelity.
- Conquest of Hindustan, 1525-1529: Panipat in April 1526, where matchlocks, chained carts, and Ustad 'Ali-quli's guns broke Sultan Ibrahim Ludi's army; the treasure of Agra given away; Khanua in March 1527 against Rana Sanga, before which Babur renounced wine, broke his gold and silver drinking cups, and called his men to fight as ghazis.
- Nature, gardens, and observation: the famous "Description of Hindustan" surveys its elephants, peacocks, monkeys, mangoes, and monsoon, then delivers the verdict that Hindustan "is a country of few charms" — no grapes, no good melons, no ice, no cold water — and elsewhere a melon brought to him in India makes him weep for home.
- The man himself: he admits weeping when a throne is lost, records his youthful infatuation with the bazaar boy Baburi, judges his own verses, catalogues the virtues and vices of dead kings and of his own begs, and describes drinking parties, ma'jun, illness, and repentance without flinching.

How to search: search for period names and Beveridge's spellings, not modern labels. The source vocabulary is Farghana, Andijan, Akhsi, Samarkand, Kabul, Hindustan, begs, Auzbeg, ma'jun, ghazi; battles are told through arrays, flanks, and standards, and years open annal-style with a Hijri date.

Treat fetched passages as evidence filtered twice: once by a king shaping his own record, once by a translator. Quote Babur's words, but distinguish them from Beveridge's bracketed insertions and footnotes, and keep interpretation separate from what the text actually states. Known limitations: there are gaps in the original manuscript record — the narrative breaks off between 1508 and 1519, again between 1520 and 1525, and stops in September 1529, more than a year before Babur died — so silence about an event is often a lost page, not a choice.
