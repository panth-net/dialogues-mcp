# Samson Occom Papers

What this corpus is: the working record of Samson Occom (1723-1792), Mohegan Presbyterian minister, schoolteacher, hymn writer, and the first Native American to publish a book in English. The genre mix is autobiography, mission journal, letter, petition, hymn, and one published execution sermon. The origin of most texts is the Occom Circle edition at Dartmouth College, which transcribes the manuscripts; the Moses Paul sermon comes from a public-domain scan of the printed pamphlet.

Occom lived inside a contradiction he never stopped narrating. He converted during the Great Awakening around 1741, studied with Eleazar Wheelock from 1743, taught and ministered at Montauk for over a decade on starvation wages, and toured England in 1766-1768 preaching hundreds of sermons to raise roughly 12,000 pounds for Wheelock's Indian Charity School. Wheelock then moved the money and the school to New Hampshire as Dartmouth College, educating English students. Occom's letters of 1771 accuse Wheelock directly; his famous line calls the college "alba mater" — a white mother — instead of alma mater.

## Where to start

- Ask why a Mohegan minister had to write his own life story — the narrative that begins "I was Born a Heathen" answers white readers who doubted his word, his wages, and his character.
- Ask what happened when Occom saw the charity money he raised turned into a college for English students — his 1771 letter tells Wheelock the school has become "alba mater," a white mother.
- Ask how he preached at the execution of Moses Paul, a fellow Mohegan, before a staring crowd — the sermon turns from the gallows to "my poor kindred" and speaks to them directly.
- Ask what he wrote while leading Christian Indians out of New England — the Brothertown journals of 1785-86 record the move onto Oneida land day by day.

The strongest material clusters:

- The autobiographical narratives of 1765 and 1768, written to answer white critics who doubted his ordination, his salary complaints, and his character. The 1768 narrative ("I was Born a Heathen") is the sharpest short account of mission-world racism written in colonial America.
- Mission journals: the early journal from the Wheelock years (1743-1748), the 1761 journey to the Oneidas, and the long 1784-1786 journals of the Brothertown migration, when Occom led New England Christian Indians to lands granted by the Oneidas in New York.
- The Wheelock correspondence, from dutiful student letters of the 1750s to the break of 1771-1772 over Moor's Charity School funds and Dartmouth's founding.
- The 1772 sermon at the execution of Moses Paul, a Mohegan convicted of murder — the first best-seller by a Native author, preaching on sin, drink, and death to a mixed audience while addressing "my poor kindred" directly.
- Brothertown and Mohegan land politics: petitions and journal entries about land, leases, tribal government, and dispossession.

How to search: use the source vocabulary of the mission world — search for names and places (Wheelock, Mohegan, Montauk, Oneida, Brothertown, London), for Occom's religious working words (preached, Sabbath, Heathen, awakened), and for the money words that run through everything (wages, charity, collections, accounts). The search-rules file maps modern concepts to this vocabulary.

How to answer from this corpus: fetch passages before making claims, quote Occom's own words, and keep his evidence separate from your interpretation. Name the document type — a journal entry, a letter to Wheelock, a petition — because Occom writes differently for himself, for patrons, and for legislatures. Known limitations: the Occom Circle transcriptions preserve erratic eighteenth-century spelling ("Sabbath Day", "Indien"), the sermon text is OCR of an eighteenth-century pamphlet with long-s errors ("fin" for "sin"), most surviving papers passed through Dartmouth — the institution Occom broke with — so the archive itself has a custodial bias, and many letters survive only as fragments.
