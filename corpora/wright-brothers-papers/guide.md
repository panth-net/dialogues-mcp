# Wilbur and Orville Wright Papers

What this corpus is: the Wright brothers explaining their own invention, in their own words, plus the survey of prior flight experiments they started from. Wilbur (1867-1912) and Orville (1871-1948) ran a print shop and then a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio; between 1899 and 1905 they worked out lift, control, and propulsion, flying the first powered, controlled airplane at Kill Devil Hills near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, on December 17, 1903. The genre mix is published lecture, magazine narrative, engineering survey, and archive catalog records for the manuscript diaries and letters.

## Where to start

- Ask for Orville's own account of December 17, 1903 — twelve seconds in a cold December wind, before spectators who came, he suspected, to see another flying machine not fly.
- Ask how two bicycle mechanics began — not with a machine but with reading, working through Chanute, Langley, and Smithsonian pamphlets before they built anything.
- Ask why they kept on when the best men before them had failed or died — their own history names Maxim's abandoned fortune and Lilienthal and Pilcher, killed in experiments.
- Ask how they thought a person learns to fly — like riding a fractious horse: you can sit on a fence and watch the birds, or you can climb on.

The downloadable, searchable core:

- "The Early History of the Airplane": three pieces by the brothers themselves — the 1908 Century Magazine account, Orville's "How We Made the First Flight" (his fullest narrative of December 14-17, 1903, from the wind of 24-27 miles an hour to the four flights, 120 feet in 12 seconds up to 852 feet in 59 seconds, and the broken skid), and Wilbur's September 1901 lecture "Some Aeronautical Experiments" to the Western Society of Engineers, given at Octave Chanute's invitation, where he works through Lilienthal's tables, glider tests at Kitty Hawk, and the control problem.
- Octave Chanute's "Progress in Flying Machines" (1894): the systematic survey of every serious flight experiment to date. This is the book the Smithsonian pointed Wilbur toward after his May 30, 1899 letter requesting reading matter, and Chanute became the brothers' chief correspondent.

The manuscript record — the pocket diaries and notebooks from the 1900-1905 Kitty Hawk and Huffman Prairie seasons, including Orville's diary entry for December 17, 1903, the Chanute correspondence, and the 1899 Smithsonian letter — lives at the Library of Congress. Those items are digitized as page images and PDFs without transcriptions, so this capsule carries them as catalog records that identify box, folder, and item but yield no passage text. Do not cite catalog records as if they were quotations; that limitation matters here.

How to search: use the brothers' working vocabulary — search for glider, soaring, lift, drift, wind, rudder, warping, tables, machine, motor, propeller, and screw; for places (Kitty Hawk, Kill Devil Hill, Dayton, Huffman) and people (Lilienthal, Chanute, Langley, Maxim, Ader). The search-rules file maps modern aviation terms onto this source vocabulary.

How to answer: fetch passages, quote the brothers directly, cite which text the words come from, and keep their evidence separate from your interpretation. Watch dates: the 1901 lecture predates success and is full of doubt; the 1908 and 1913 accounts are retrospective and shaped for public argument about priority.
