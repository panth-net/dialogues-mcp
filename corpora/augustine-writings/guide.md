# Augustine of Hippo Writings

What this corpus is: the core first-person and public writings of Augustine (354-430), bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa. The genre mix matters. The *Confessions* (written c. 397-400) is a prayer addressed to God that became the first great introspective autobiography: Thagaste and his mother Monica, the stolen pears of Book II, the Manichaean years and the disappointing Faustus, Ambrose in Milan, the garden scene of Book VIII with the child's voice chanting "take up and read," the death of the unnamed friend in Book IV, the vision at Ostia and Monica's death in Book IX, and then the turn inward and upward — memory in Book X, time in Book XI. *The City of God* (413-426) is public argument on the largest scale: after Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410 and refugees blamed Christianity for the disaster, Augustine answered with twenty-two books contrasting the earthly city and the City of God. The letters are the working correspondence of a bishop: disputes with Jerome over the interpretation of Galatians, the long grind of the Donatist controversy, Pelagianism, consolation, and church discipline.

## Where to start

- Ask why the theft of a few pears bothered Augustine for the rest of his life — he stole fruit he didn't even want, and that was the part he couldn't explain.
- Ask what happened when the friend he loved died young — grief made his own hometown unbearable, and he calls the dead man "half of my soul."
- Ask about the prayer "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet" — the most honest sentence ever written about wanting to change without wanting it now.
- Ask him what memory is — he wanders the "fields and spacious palaces" of his own mind and is astonished that he cannot contain himself.

The strongest material clusters:

- Conversion and the divided will: Books VI-VIII of the *Confessions* — "grant me chastity and continency, only not yet," Ponticianus's story of Antony of Egypt, the fig tree and the voice in the garden.
- Memory and time: Book X's vast "fields and palaces of memory"; Book XI's "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know" and the threefold present.
- Grief and friendship: the friend's death at Thagaste in Book IV, Adeodatus, and Monica at Ostia in Book IX.
- The sack of Rome and the two cities: the opening books of *The City of God* against the pagan accusation, and the theology of two loves building two cities.
- Pastoral letters: Jerome, Nebridius, Donatists, and the daily governance of a North African see.

How to search: search for concrete source vocabulary — proper names (Monica, Ambrose, Alypius, Faustus, Ponticianus, Jerome, Alaric, Rome, Carthage, Milan, Ostia, Hippo), the translations' 19th-century religious diction (Thou, concupiscence, continency, manifold, vouchsafe), and Augustine's own recurring words (memory, time, will, grace, rest, weight, love). Modern terms like "psychology" or "identity" will miss the text; translate them into his vocabulary first.

How to answer: quote fetched passages as evidence, cite them, and separate the text from your interpretation. Always say which work and, where visible, which book the passage comes from — Book II's pears and Book XI's time are doing different work. Keep the languages straight: the English is Pusey's 1838 translation of the *Confessions* and Dods's 1871 translation of *The City of God*, both Victorian in diction; the Latin *Confessiones* is included so translation claims can be checked against Augustine's actual words (fetch the parallel Latin passage when a claim hangs on one English word). Known limitations: the translations are 19th-century and shape the tone; the letters volume is uncorrected OCR with degraded searchability; and this corpus omits the sermons, the anti-Pelagian treatises, and *On the Trinity*, so it cannot represent the whole of Augustine's thought.
