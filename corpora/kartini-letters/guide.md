# Kartini Letters

Use this corpus to read colonial Java from inside the regency house at Japara. The genre is personal correspondence, and what this corpus is: between 25 May 1899 and her death on 17 September 1904, Raden Adjeng Kartini (1879-1904), daughter of Raden Mas Adipati Ario Sosroningrat, Regent of Japara, wrote long Dutch letters to pen friends and patrons — Stella Zeehandelaar, the Amsterdam feminist she addressed before ever meeting her; Rosa Abendanon-Mandri, her "Moedertje"; Mevrouw Ovink-Soer; the socialist member of parliament H.H. van Kol and Nellie van Kol; Professor Anton of Jena and his wife. Two editions are indexed side by side: the Dutch collection "Door Duisternis tot Licht" (1911) and the shorter English translation "Letters of a Javanese Princess" (1920).

## Where to start

- Ask what she means when she calls her own home "the cage" — locked in at twelve to wait for marriage, she keeps writing about wings anyway. Most of the letters are Dutch; ask for her words and a translation together.
- Ask who Stella was, and why Kartini poured her whole self out to a woman in Amsterdam she never once met.
- Ask whether she should go to Holland to study — you can hear her arguing with her own dream, already counting what it will cost her.
- Ask what she and her sisters were reading behind the regency walls — books were the one world allowed in.

The strongest material clusters:

- Seclusion and freedom: taken out of the European school at twelve and locked inside the regency to await marriage, she writes of the cage (kooi), of wings, and of wanting to be free; her sisters Roekmini and Kardinah — the "klaverblad", the cloverleaf — share the confinement and the plans.
- Education and the school plan: the dream of training as a teacher, the hope of studying in Holland, van Kol's push for a government scholarship, the renunciation of that scholarship in 1903, and the little school for regents' daughters she and Roekmini actually opened at Japara.
- Colonial Java: Dutch officials and rank etiquette, the "ethical policy" years, opium and the people's poverty, what a Javanese noblewoman may and may not do, and the double position of loving Dutch culture while indicting Dutch rule.
- Marriage and polygamy: her hatred of polygamy, argued to Stella from the first letters; her father's household; the 1903 engagement and marriage to Raden Adipati Djojo Adiningrat, Regent of Rembang, a widower with other wives and children; and the changed, quieter tone of the last letters — she died four days after her son Soesalit was born.
- Javanese art and culture: gamelan evenings at the regency, batik (she wrote on it for the 1898 National Exhibition of Women's Work in The Hague), the wood-carvers of Japara she promoted, wajang stories, and Javanese belief explained for Dutch readers.

How to search: search for the source vocabulary in the right language. The Dutch letters say onderwijs, vrouwen, vrijheid, kooi, huwelijk, Regent, Javaan; the English translation says education, women, freedom, cage, marriage. A passage found in one edition may be missing from the other, so try both languages before concluding anything is absent.

Treat fetched passages as evidence shaped twice before it reaches you: Kartini writes performatively for particular Dutch readers, and J.H. Abendanon selected and cut the letters for publication with his own protective bias. Quote exactly, say which edition the quote comes from, and keep interpretation separate from what the letter states. Known limitations: the English edition omits about a third of the Dutch collection, the Dutch collection is itself a curated selection, and the full unabridged correspondence was only published decades later and is not included here.
