# Fujiwara no Teika Diary (Meigetsuki)

This capsule is catalog-only. No passage text is installed until you import a permitted transcription yourself. The manifest records point to museum records for surviving autograph sections — manuscript images, not text — so searches return nothing until an import has been run.

What this corpus is: Meigetsuki, "Record of the Clear Moon" (明月記), the diary Fujiwara no Teika (1162-1241) kept for some 56 years, from 1180 to 1235 — from the outbreak of the Genpei War, when the eighteen-year-old famously wrote that the red banners and campaigns against the barbarians were no concern of his, to his old age as the most influential poet-editor in the history of Japanese verse. Its origin is the Kyoto court; its genre is a courtier's daily diary written in kanbun, the Classical Chinese of Japanese officialdom, in Teika's famously idiosyncratic hand. Parts survive as his autograph (much of it in the Reizei family library, with sections in national museums); the rest survives in later copies.

## Where to start

- Ask what survives of the diary Teika kept for 56 years, and what those years held. No passage text is installed, but the surviving-manuscript records and the diary's world — a red aurora over Kyoto, court promotion lists, a poet's failing body — can be discussed now.
- Ask how to unlock the diary itself — importing a permitted transcription is what turns this capsule from a catalog into searchable text in Teika's own words.

The strongest material clusters:

- The sky: the red vapor (赤気) he recorded over successive nights in early 1204, now read as a great auroral storm; and the guest-star (客星) list of 1230, when Teika had the yin-yang office search precedent records and copied out eight historical guest stars — including the 1054 star near Tianguan that modern astronomers cite from Meigetsuki when dating the Crab Nebula supernova. Comets, eclipses, and omens run through the diary.
- Court career: decades of promotion anxiety — appointment lists scrutinized, patrons courted, the 1185 lamp-throwing incident that briefly cost him his post, the ban from Retired Emperor Go-Toba's palace poetry gatherings in 1220, and the Jokyu War of 1221 that exiled Go-Toba and reversed Teika's fortunes; he finally reached provisional middle counselor in 1232, at seventy.
- Poetry and canon: the commission to compile the Shin Kokinshu in 1201, poetry contests and disputes with Go-Toba, sole compilation of the Shin Chokusenshu in the 1230s, and the selection at his Ogura villa that became the Hyakunin Isshu.
- The body: Teika's terrible health complaints — coughing fits, fevers, aching limbs, insomnia — recorded as insistently as any court ceremony, alongside fires, famines, and the weather of Kyoto.

How to search, once text is imported: search for the source vocabulary of a kanbun court diary — 赤気, 客星, 彗星, 除目, 歌合, 病 — or the corresponding terms of whatever translation or transcription you imported. Entries are dated by era name (Kennin, Jokyu, Kanki), month, and day; fetch neighboring days around any event.

How to answer: quote fetched passages, cite the era-name date, and keep the diary's evidence separate from interpretation. Limitations to state plainly: the text survives partly in autograph and partly in later copies, and famous passages — including the red-banners line, preserved only in copies — carry real uncertainty about later editing; the original is kanbun, so any modern Japanese or English rendering is a translation; and the astronomical entries mix observation with omen-reading, so date conversions to the Julian calendar need care. Catalog records of manuscript images are never passage evidence.
