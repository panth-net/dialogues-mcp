# Evliya Celebi Travels

Use this corpus to travel the mid-seventeenth-century Ottoman world with its most talkative eyewitness. The genre is the seyahatname, the Ottoman book of travels, and what this corpus is: the first two volumes of the old English translation by Joseph von Hammer (1834 and 1850) of the ten-volume Seyahatname of Evliya Celebi (1611-1682), son of the chief court goldsmith Dervish Mehmed Zilli. In 1630 Evliya dreamed the Prophet stood before him in a mosque by the harbor; meaning to beg for intercession (shifa'at), he stammered "travel" (siyahat) instead — and was granted both. He spent roughly forty years wandering three continents and wrote it all down.

## Where to start

- Ask about the dream that gave him permission to leave home. Meaning to beg the Prophet for intercession, he stammered "travel" instead — and was granted both.
- Ask how a goldsmith's son ended up inside the Sultan's palace. Murad IV heard him recite the whole Koran in eight hours one night in Aya Sofya and took him in among the royal pages.
- Ask about the talismans of Istanbul. He inventories enchanted columns and bronze demons as gravely as gates and walls, and whether you believe him is half the pleasure.
- Ask about the storm on the Black Sea that nearly ended the whole book at sea. Waves stood up like walls, the cargo went over the side, and Evliya survived to embellish the tale.

The strongest material clusters:

- Istanbul described (vol. I): sayings of the Prophet about Constantinople, foundation legends, the circuit of the walls and the paces between the twenty-seven gates, the sixty-six talismans of the city, nine sieges before the Ottomans, and Mehmed II's conquest of 1453.
- Sultans and the court (vol. I): the imperial mosques from Bayezid II to Sultan Ahmed, and reign-by-reign rosters of vezirs, muftis, and ulema from Suleyman to Mehmed IV — including Murad IV, who heard the young Evliya recite in Aya Sofya and took him into the palace, and the character and fall of Sultan Ibrahim.
- Roads and journeys (vol. II): Bursa with its hot springs, market, and ice-worm; Nicomedia; Sinope and Samsun on the sea road to Trebizond (Trabzon); Georgia and Mingrelia; the land of the Abaza; the Crimea of the Tatar khans; Erzurum and its fortress; and the mission into Persia to Tabriz, its colleges, and the tomb of Shems Tabrizi.
- War and the frontier (vol. II): the Azov (Azak) campaign, an inroad into Mingrelia, the 1645 expedition against Crete and the siege of Canea, and a Black Sea storm on the return from the Crimea that nearly ended the whole book at sea.
- Marvels and tall tales: enchanted columns, talismans against serpents and plague, saints' miracles, and gloriously implausible numbers. Treat these as genre features — the wonders ('ajaib) an Ottoman reader expected — evidence of what was told, believed, and enjoyed, not failed reporting to be silently corrected.

How to search: search for Hammer's spellings and Ottoman court vocabulary. The source vocabulary is Sultan, vezir, mufti, aghas, janissaries, dervish, sheikh, mosque, fortress, talisman, and place names in their 1830s dress: Trebisonde, Erzerum, Azak, Brussa, Tabriz.

Treat fetched passages as evidence at two removes: Evliya wrote up his notes years after the road, delighting in embellishment, and von Hammer's nineteenth-century translation is dated and sometimes loose. Quote what the text says, attribute wonders to Evliya's telling rather than to fact, and keep interpretation separate from the claims themselves. Known limitations: these two volumes are a small fraction of the ten — eight further volumes, from Syria to Vienna to Mecca to Egypt, were never translated in this series, so absence here says nothing about what Evliya saw.
