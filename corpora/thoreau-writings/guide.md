# Corpus Guide

## What this corpus is

This corpus is for Thoreau as a working observer, correspondent, essayist, walker, and moral reasoner. It uses journals, familiar letters, and selected public essays from public-source editions.

Read it as evidence of how he turns daily seeing into thought: weather, plants, work, walking, reading, money, household life, friendship, conscience, reform, and literary self-fashioning.

## Where to start

- Ask about the night he spent in jail rather than pay his tax — and what changed, and what did not, when he walked out the next morning because someone else paid it for him.
- Ask what he actually did all day at Walden — hoeing beans, turning up arrowheads in the field, selling the crop; the experiment was labor before it was philosophy.
- Ask what the house by the pond cost him — he gives the exact figure, item by item, to prove a point about how little a life really requires.
- Ask what he wrote to a friend after his brother John died — "What right have I to grieve, who have not ceased to wonder?" — grief handled the only way he knew, by thinking it through.

## How to talk with this corpus

Do not reduce Thoreau to a single slogan about nature, solitude, or civil disobedience. Ask what problem a passage is solving: attention, economy, conscience, observation, friendship, public speech, or the conversion of experience into prose.

Separate journal entry, letter, and later public essay. A diary note is not the same kind of evidence as a polished lecture or published essay.

## How to search

Start with concrete source words: pond, walk, woods, field, ice, beans, house, labor, economy, conscience, state, slave, war, friendship, letter, Emerson, Blake, Concord, Maine, morning, spring, winter.

For broad questions, search across both everyday vocabulary and conceptual vocabulary. Then fetch passages around the hits before making claims about the pattern of mind.

## How to answer

Use fetched passages as the evidence. Explain whether the answer is coming from a journal, letter, or published essay. If the local corpus uses edited public-domain editions, mention that editorial selection and modernization may affect what survives.

Quote sparingly. Thoreau often sounds aphoristic; do not let one sharp sentence stand in for the whole archive.

## What to watch for

- Treating later fame as proof that every passage has the same doctrine.
- Ignoring date and genre.
- Turning field observation into generic "nature writing."
- Treating edited public-domain editions as complete manuscript evidence.
- Forgetting that letters and journals show social life, work, illness, and money as well as solitude.
