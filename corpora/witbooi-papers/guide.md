# Hendrik Witbooi Papers

## What this corpus is

Use this corpus to read the German colonization of South West Africa (Namibia) through the man who refused it in writing. Hendrik Witbooi (c. 1830-1905) succeeded his father Moses as kaptein of the Witbooi Nama at Gibeon in 1884. He kept his outgoing letters, incoming letters, treaty texts, and diary notes in a leather-bound journal bought from Barry Arnold & Co., stationers of Cape Town: 366 pages, 189 of them filled, first entry 18 June 1884, last entry 6 April 1893 written in red ink. Six days later, on 12 April 1893, German troops under Major Curt von Francois stormed his stronghold at Hoornkrans and carried the book off. The genre is a working chancery record, not a private confession: Witbooi wrote to be read by kapteins, missionaries, magistrates, and German officers, and he kept copies because words were his legal record. The corpus indexes the 1929 Van Riebeeck Society edition of that captured letter-book, which also prints his correspondence through the Naukluft campaign of 1894.

The strongest material clusters:

- The warning letters to Maharero and Samuel Maharero of the Herero, above all the letter arguing that accepting German "protection" means surrender: "u zult eeuwig berouw hebben, dat u uwe land en regeringsreg in de handen der witte menschen afgegeven hebben" (you will eternally regret giving your land and right of rule into the hands of the white men).
- Exchanges with German officials — Commissioner Heinrich Göring, Major Curt von Francois, and Governor Theodor Leutwein — in which Witbooi refuses the Schutzvertrag (protection treaty), questions by what right a foreign power claims Namaland, and answers threats point by point.
- Letters to Nama leaders such as Jan Jonker Afrikaner, defending his wars as lawful and himself as "geen moordenaar" (no murderer), and his long war and eventual peace with the Herero.
- The Hoornkrans years: peace with the Herero in 1892, the German attack of 12 April 1893, and letters written from the field afterward, including appeals to Cape officials that the Germans "voor oorlog klaar in dit land gekomen" (came into this land ready for war).
- The Naukluft (Naauwkloof) letters of May to August 1894: correspondence with Leutwein during the mountain campaign that ended with Witbooi's surrender on 15 September 1894.
- His self-understanding: he signed himself kaptein and at times "Koning van Groot Namakwaland," and he reasons constantly from God's will, treaties, and law rather than from mere force.

## Where to start

- Ask what Witbooi told the Herero chief about accepting German "protection" — one African leader warning another, in writing, that handing your land and right of rule to the white men means eternal regret. He wrote in Cape Dutch; ask for his words with a translation.
- Ask why he refused the protection treaty the Germans pressed on every captain — he saw the trap in it and said so to their faces.
- Ask why he believed the chiefs should stand together "for our land, Africa" — a plea he sent to a rival he had been at war with for years.
- Ask how he answers when his wars are called murder — he argues the difference between war and murder like a lawyer, from treaties, promises, and God's will.

## How to talk with this corpus

Witbooi's letters are moral-legal argument. Ask what claim each letter makes: about land, about the difference between a war between neighbors and a foreign conquest, about who may punish whom, about promises and treaties. Keep the addressee visible — a letter to Leutwein does different work than a letter to a fellow kaptein.

Treat the edition's frame with suspicion. The 1929 edition wraps Witbooi's own words in a hostile colonial apparatus: an English introduction and chronology that call his 1904 rising treacherous, and a foreword memoir by the German trader Gustav Voigts full of period judgments and the term "Hottentot." That apparatus is useful context and searchable here, but it is evidence about settler opinion, not about Witbooi's mind. Quote Witbooi from the numbered letters; quote the introduction only as the editors' and settlers' view, and say so.

Known limitations: Witbooi's own texts here run 1884-1894. His final rising of October 1904 and his death near Vaalgras on 29 October 1905 appear only in the editorial introduction and chronology, not in his own words. The transcription is diplomatic, keeping his nonstandard Cape Dutch spelling, and page markers from the digitization share the text stream with the letters.

## How to search

Search in Dutch first; this is Cape Dutch with Nama-inflected, phonetic spelling, and the editorial apparatus is in Afrikaans and English. Use source vocabulary: names of people (Leutwein, Francois, Goering, Maharero, Jan Jonker Afrikaner, Samuel Isaak), places (Gibeon, Hoornkrans, Naauwkloof, Windhoek, Rehoboth, Walvisbaai), and Witbooi's own key words — land, regeringsreg (right of rule), bescherming (protection), traktaat, vrede (peace), oorlog (war), schietgoed (ammunition). If a modern spelling returns nothing, try period spellings: Naauwkloof for Naukluft, Duitsche or Duitsters for German.

## How to answer

Fetch passages before making claims, quote Witbooi's Dutch with an English gloss, and cite the letter number and date visible in the passage. Separate three voices in every answer: Witbooi's letters, the Afrikaans editorial footnotes, and the English introduction. Distinguish evidence from interpretation, and say plainly when a question — especially about 1904-1905 — falls outside what this edition lets Witbooi say for himself.
