# Xu Xiake Travel Diaries

What this corpus is: the Xu Xiake Youji (徐霞客遊記), the travel diaries of Xu Xiake (1587-1641), a gentleman of Jiangyin who spent roughly thirty years walking China and writing up each day on the road. The genre is the youji, the travel record, but Xu pushed it somewhere new: entries log route, distances in li, lodging, weather, and terrain, and then stop to measure — how deep a cave runs, where a river actually rises, how one ridge connects to the next. The result reads like field notebooks centuries ahead of their time.

## Where to start

The diaries are in classical Chinese — ask in English and the AI finds and translates the passages for you.

- Ask what it is like to crawl into an unmapped cave with one torch. He presses in flat against the rock, half in water, wondering who will pass the torch through if he has to swim — and keeps going.
- Ask about the monk Jingwen, who died on the road south. Xu carried his friend's ashes a thousand miles more to bury them on the mountain Jingwen had vowed to reach.
- Ask about climbing Huangshan when the trails had been snowed shut for two months. The man sent up with supplies turned back in waist-deep snow; Xu went up.
- Ask why he decided the classics were wrong about where the Yangtze begins. Everyone else cited the ancient texts; Xu walked the rivers and trusted his feet.

The strongest material clusters:

- The early mountain diaries: Tiantai (the famous opening diary of 1613), Yandang, Huangshan (climbed in 1616 and again in 1618), Wuyi, and the pilgrimage peaks.
- The great southwest expedition of 1636-1640: out through Zhejiang and Jiangxi to Hunan, then Guangxi, Guizhou, and Yunnan — more than half the surviving text.
- Karst country: the limestone towers around Guilin and Yangshuo, and cave after cave entered with torches, paced out, and described chamber by chamber. This is proto-speleology done on foot.
- Hydrography: the embedded treatises and diary arguments, above all the case that the Jinsha River (金沙江), not the Min River of the classical Yugong tradition, is the true upper course of the Yangtze, plus his untangling of the Pan River system.
- The human record: travel with his servant Gu Xing and the monk Jingwen, who was wounded when they were robbed on the Xiang River in 1637 and died in Nanning; Xu carried his ashes on to Jizu Mountain (雞足山) in Yunnan. Near the end Gu Xing deserted him; Xu's legs failed in Yunnan in 1640, he was carried home, and he died in 1641.

How to search: after download, this corpus holds sixteen curated chapters from Chinese Wikisource in traditional characters — the early mountain diaries, the core southwest diaries, and the river-source treatises. Search for concrete source vocabulary — place names (桂林, 麗江, 永昌), terrain words (洞, 巖, 峰, 江), personal names (靜聞, 顧僕) — rather than modern abstractions. Write terms naturally (`黃山` works as-is) and use traditional forms — simplified characters usually return nothing.

When answering, quote the diary passage, cite it, and separate the evidence from interpretation: Xu records what he paced and saw, and his inferences are usually explicit, so keep his observation distinct from his conclusion and from modern geography. Known limitations: the download is a curated selection, not the complete Youji, so absence from search is weak evidence; the diaries were compiled after his death by Ji Mengliang in 1642, parts were lost in the Ming-Qing wars and reconstructed from copies, so gaps and editorial patching are real; the Wikisource transcription is community-proofread, and bracketed footnote numbers plus the notes at chapter end are Wikisource editorial apparatus, not diary text; dates run on the Chinese calendar in reign years (Chongzhen), not the Western calendar.
