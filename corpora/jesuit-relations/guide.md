# Corpus Guide

## What this corpus is

The Jesuit Relations and Allied Documents is a 73-volume collection edited by Reuben Gold Thwaites and published from 1896 to 1901. It contains French, English, Latin, and Italian missionary reports, letters, and related documents about New France from 1610 to 1791.

The corpus is especially useful for questions about missionary work, colonial administration, Indigenous diplomacy, councils, kinship, religious encounters, trade, warfare, settlement, illness, travel, and everyday constraints.

## Where to start

- Ask what a priest's first winter inside a Montagnais household was like — months of cold, near-blinding smoke, and shared hunger in the cabin, counted out plainly by the man who lived it.
- Ask what each side made of the other's medicine when someone fell sick — one healer answers the priests directly: "That is very good for you people; but, for us, it is thus that we cure our sick."
- Ask who found whom ugly at first meeting — the priests record being told, more than once, that the French beards were the offense.
- Ask how the deaths of Brébeuf and Lalemant were reported back to France — accounts written as letters home, for readers deciding whether to mourn the mission or join it.

## What it can answer

Use this corpus for source-grounded questions about what Jesuit missionaries and editors recorded. It can help surface descriptions of Indigenous governance, missionary interpretation, language encounters, debates about freedom and obedience, diplomacy, and daily life.

## What it cannot answer

The corpus cannot directly represent Indigenous thought without mediation. Indigenous voices are usually filtered through Jesuit authors, translators, editors, and OCR. Absence of a term is not proof that an idea was absent. Modern concepts may not appear under modern names.

## Recommended workflow

Do not answer only from memory. Read this guide, search with corpus-specific vocabulary, fetch promising passages with context, then synthesize. Treat fetched passages as evidence and distinguish that evidence from interpretation.

## How to talk with this corpus

Do not treat the corpus as a lookup database. Treat it as a body of texts with voice, context, omissions, contradictions, and recurring concerns. Explain old or strange language in plain English. Mention missionary and colonial bias when it matters.

Use source-language terms only when they matter for interpretation. If a word such as `conseil`, `capitaine`, `chef`, `coutume`, `loi`, `liberte`, or `sauvage` carries meaning that modern English would flatten, gloss it briefly and move back to the user's question.

## How to bring this corpus to life

Look for recurring worries, practical constraints, repeated metaphors, contradictions, assumptions the authors do not explain, silences, changes over time, and moments of tension, intimacy, comedy, fear, pride, or confusion.

## Response style

A good answer should answer the user directly, use fetched evidence, quote selectively, cite every source passage, explain historical vocabulary, separate evidence from interpretation, mention uncertainty or gaps, and suggest better follow-up searches when useful. Keep translation or language caveats brief unless the user asks about wording.

## Suggested first searches

- `council OR conseil OR assembly`
- `liberty OR liberte OR freedom OR libre`
- `chief OR captain OR capitaine OR chef`
- `custom OR coutume OR law OR loi`
- `trade OR commerce OR beaver OR castor`
