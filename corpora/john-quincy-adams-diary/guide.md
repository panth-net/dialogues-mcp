# John Quincy Adams Diary

The searchable text here is the twelve-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams (edited by his son Charles Francis Adams, 1874-1877), public-domain OCR downloaded from the Internet Archive. It is a selection — roughly two-fifths of the diary, chosen and trimmed by a Victorian editor — so an entry you expect may simply not be in it. The complete modern transcription, the JQA Digital Diary at the Primary Source Cooperative (MHS), is a catalog-only pointer: consult it online when the Memoirs omit a date or the OCR looks doubtful.

What this corpus is: the diary John Quincy Adams began in November 1779, aged twelve, on a wartime voyage to Europe with his father, and kept until days before his death in February 1848 — 51 manuscript volumes over 68 years, the longest continuous inside account of the early American republic. The genre is a private daily diary that doubled as a working political record: he wrote up negotiations, cabinet fights, and House debates the same evenings he scolded himself for indolence.

## Where to start

- Ask what a former president wrote about himself in private — "My whole life has been a succession of disappointments" is his own verdict, set down by a man who had held nearly every office the republic offered.
- Ask about the dawn swims in the Potomac — an hour in the river before sunrise, logged as faithfully as cabinet fights.
- Ask how one old congressman broke the gag rule — for eight years the House refused to hear antislavery petitions, and Adams kept forcing them in until the rule fell.
- Ask what 68 years of daily entries do to a man — he told his sons to consult "this record of their father's life," and you can watch him measure himself decade by decade.

The strongest material clusters:

- Diplomacy: The Hague and Berlin in the 1790s, minister to Russia at St. Petersburg from 1809, the Treaty of Ghent negotiations with Britain in 1814.
- Secretary of State under Monroe, 1817-1825: the Adams-Onis Treaty of 1819 and the cabinet arguments behind the Monroe Doctrine of 1823.
- The presidency, 1825-1829, won in the House after the bitter 1824 election and shadowed by the "corrupt bargain" charge.
- The House antislavery years, 1831-1848: presenting petitions by the thousand, fighting the gag rule from 1836 until its repeal in December 1844, arguing for the Amistad captives before the Supreme Court in February-March 1841, and facing down a censure attempt in 1842.
- The private Adams: brutal self-assessments ("My whole life has been a succession of disappointments"), dawn swims in the Potomac, Bible reading, and a passion for astronomy — the "light-houses of the skies" observatories he urged on Congress, and the Smithson bequest he shepherded.

How to search: search for the concrete vocabulary of the period and of Adams's own habits — `petition`, `gag`, `Amistad`, `Ghent`, `despatch`, `observatory`, `swim`, `indolence` — not modern labels. He is formal, Latinate, and exact about names and dates. The text is uncorrected OCR of 1870s typography, so if a term returns nothing, try a variant spelling or a nearby word before concluding it is absent.

How to answer: quote from fetched passages, cite the entry date and the Memoirs volume, and keep evidence separate from interpretation. Adams writing about Jackson, Clay, or Calhoun is testimony from a combatant, not a neutral record. Known limitations: the Memoirs are Charles Francis Adams's selection, not the whole diary, with the editor's cuts and discretion; the OCR is uncorrected and mangles names and numbers; Adams often jotted short daily lines and expanded them into long entries later, so a day can exist in two versions; late entries after his 1846 stroke are thin; and catalog metadata is never passage evidence. If the needed entry is not in the Memoirs selection, say so and point to the Digital Diary online rather than reconstructing it from memory.
