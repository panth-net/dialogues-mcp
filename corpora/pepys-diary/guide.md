# Samuel Pepys Diary

Use this corpus to read Restoration London through one unusually honest observer. Pepys kept his diary in shorthand from January 1660 to May 1669, while working his way up the Navy Office. The genre is a private daily diary written for himself alone, which is exactly why it matters: he recorded state business and private life in the same entry — a Privy Council meeting, then a play, then a quarrel with his wife, then his monthly accounting of what he is "worth."

## Where to start

- Ask how he spent the week London burned — the entry where he and Sir W. Pen dig pits in the garden for the wine and the Parmazan cheese shows you exactly what a man grabs when everything is on fire.
- Ask what it was like to keep going to the office through the plague summer of 1665, with houses shut up all around him — at year's end he admits he "never lived so merrily," or made so much money, as in the plague time.
- Ask what he did on the last day of every month — he cast up his accounts, wrote down exactly what he was "worth," and blessed God for the number, half prayer and half bookkeeping.
- Ask how the affair with his wife's companion Deb Willet came apart — his wife walked in on them, and he wrote the whole disaster down against himself, lies and shame included.

The strongest material clusters:

- The 1665 plague year: deaths in the parish, shut houses, flight from the city, his own fear and business-as-usual.
- The Great Fire of September 1666: the famous eyewitness account, burying his parmesan cheese and wine, advising the King.
- Navy Office work during the Dutch wars: contracts, victualling, pay, the Medway disaster of 1667, defending himself before Parliament.
- Marriage and household: Elizabeth, servants, jealousy, reconciliations, and the Deb Willet affair told against himself.
- Pleasure and self-management: theatre, music, food, vows to drink less and see fewer plays, fines he paid himself for breaking them.

Treat fetched entries as evidence of an evening reconstruction, not a neutral record. Pepys is candid but still curates; he writes for himself, in shorthand, with incriminating passages in a mix of languages. Known limitations: the Wheatley edition used here silently omits some explicit passages, so absence of a topic in a hit is not proof Pepys never wrote it, and editorial footnotes share the text stream with the diary itself.

Dates matter enormously in this corpus. Entries are dated headers in old-style years. When a question is about an event, search for the event vocabulary first, then fetch neighboring context to walk day by day.
